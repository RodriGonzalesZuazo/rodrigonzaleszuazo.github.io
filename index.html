About Me

I am an environmental economist at Conservation Strategy Fund, a leading organization in conservation economics. My research seeks to understand the economic drivers of tropical deforestation and its feedbacks into the economy, combining econometric methods with remotely sensed data. Bolivia and its unique ecosystems are my primary empirical setting.

At CSF I lead studies on the economics of land use and non-market valuation, and support climate and conservation finance initiatives with national and international partners.

I have also done research for Purdue University, running household fieldwork for a post-RCT conservation study in Bolivia's rural lowlands that grew into my master's thesis. Earlier, I was a researcher on trade and labor markets in developing countries within a Swiss National Science Foundation project, working heavily with microdata econometrics, which led to the Central Bank of Bolivia's Best Research Paper Award.

My policy work includes serving as Principal Economist for the Real Sector at Bolivia's Ministry of Economy and Public Finance, working on macroeconomic policy. Separately, I have consulted for international organizations, including UNDP and the IDB, on biodiversity data and sustainable finance.

I lived in Boston for two years. Aside from discovering Dunkin' and lobster rolls, I earned an MA in Sustainable International Development at Brandeis University, fully funded by the Government of Japan and the World Bank as one of two Latin Americans selected in a global cohort of more than 120 scholars.

Current Research

I am currently developing causal research designs that combine remotely sensed data with econometric methods to measure the economic and ecological impacts of deforestation in Bolivia's globally significant ecosystems.

Bolivia holds a rare biogeographic mosaic. Within a single country, the southwestern rim of the Amazon basin meets the Chiquitano dry forest, the largest intact tropical dry forest on Earth, and transitions into the Gran Chaco and the Bolivian Pantanal, producing sharp ecological gradients over short distances. This is also one of the world's fastest-moving agricultural frontiers. Bolivia now loses more primary forest than any country except Brazil, as soy and cattle expansion collide with some of the least-studied forests on the planet. That combination of ecological uniqueness, rapid land-use change, and scarce rigorous evidence makes Bolivia an exceptional natural laboratory for understanding what deforestation does to ecosystems and to the economies that depend on them.

Publications

Working papers

Productivity, Deforestation, and the Extensive Trap: A Structural Macro Model for Bolivia

Presented at the Conference on Nature and the Macroeconomy, Central Bank of Chile – CETEx (LSE) – SUREAL (U. Zurich), Santiago de Chile, 2026

Abstract

This paper develops a structural macro-ecological model (D-ESTEEM) for the Bolivian economy to study how primary forest loss feeds back into productivity and the external sector. Deforestation expands agricultural land while eroding the ecosystem services that sustain yields, pushing the economy into an extensive trap in which growth relies on further land conversion rather than productivity gains. The key ecological elasticity is micro-founded with a municipal panel from Santa Cruz combining satellite land-cover data and panel econometrics. Policy scenarios trace the macroeconomic and fiscal consequences of alternative land-use paths.

The Relationship Between Deforestation and Productivity in Farmlands in Bolivia

Mimeo prepared for Fundación FAUTAPO

Abstract

Using a panel of 56 municipalities in Santa Cruz over 2013–2024, this study examines how deforestation relates to agricultural productivity, combining MapBiomas land-cover data with two-way fixed effects and Mundlak decompositions, validated against satellite vegetation indices. The results document significant yield losses associated with forest loss, challenging the assumption that agricultural expansion into forest land is productivity-enhancing.

Cultural Consensus on Environmental Conservation Programs: Mechanisms Behind a Randomized Controlled Trial in Rural Bolivia

Master's thesis, Brandeis University, 2025

Abstract

This thesis studies the mechanisms behind a conservation-payment randomized controlled trial (Acuerdos Recíprocos por Agua) in the Bolivian subtropical Andes. Drawing on original household survey data, it applies Cultural Consensus Analysis to measure how communities understand conservation agreements and how that shared understanding relates to program outcomes.
